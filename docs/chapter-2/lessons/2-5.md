## 2. Goals and prerequisites

Goals
1. Say whether a whole number is even or odd (pairs; ones digit 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 = even).
2. Know and use the three sum patterns: even + even = even; odd + odd = even; even + odd = odd (either order).
3. Explain why: pairs and a "leftover one"; only the ones digits decide, because tens and hundreds are always even.
4. Fill a missing *even/odd* word in a pattern sentence and write their own 3-digit equation that fits it.
5. Use the pattern to check a sum: a sum with the wrong even/odd is surely wrong; a sum with the right even/odd still has to be added to be sure.
6. Apply to real-world totals, including three addends.

Prerequisites: adding 3-digit numbers (sums to 999), place value (2-1), even/odd from Grade 2 (pairs).

## 3. Vocabulary

- **Even number** — a number you can split into pairs with none left over. Its ones digit is 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8.
- **Odd number** — a number that has one left over when you make pairs. Its ones digit is 1, 3, 5, 7, or 9.
- **Pattern** — something that happens the same way every time.
- **Sum** — the answer when you add.
- **Addend** — a number you add.
- **Ones digit** — the digit in the ones place (the last digit).

## 4. Methods taught in Learn

1. **Pairs model (why).** Even = all in pairs. Odd = pairs plus one leftover. Odd + odd: the two leftovers make a new pair → even. Even + odd: one leftover is still alone → odd. Even + even: no leftovers → even. (Answers the book's "Math is… Generalizations" question.)
2. **Ones digit test.** For any number, look at the ones digit. For a sum, only the ones digits of the addends matter: hundreds and tens are groups of ten, and ten is even, so they never leave a leftover. (Book items 9 and 10.)
3. **Adding the same even number keeps the type** (Be Curious): 3, 7, 11, 15… adding 4 each time stays odd because odd + even = odd every time.
4. **Pattern sentences** (book Learn boxes and On My Own 1–6): fill the missing word; support with two 3-digit examples.
5. **Use patterns to check a sum** (Learn closing line, Work Together, 7–8, Reflect): predict even/odd first, then add. If the sum's type does not match the prediction, the sum is wrong. If it does match, that alone does **not** prove it right (Nisha: 135 + 232 = 167 is odd as predicted, but the real sum is 367).

### Step 2 — Even + even, odd + odd
Example parts:
1. Pairs picture 4 + 6: both all pairs → total all pairs → **even**.
2. Pairs picture 3 + 5: each has one leftover; the two leftovers join into a new pair → **even**.
3. "It works for big numbers too: 214 + 352 = 566 (even + even = even). 135 + 221 = 356 (odd + odd = even)."

Your Turn: `parts`, two `choice` parts (choices "even", "odd"):

Both answers "even". Shuffle part order with rng so the child cannot learn "first = even". Hint: "Even + even: no leftovers. Odd + odd: the two leftovers make a pair." Explanation gives both exact sums.

### Step 3 — Even + odd (and the cube trains)
Example parts:
1. Pairs picture 4 + 3: one leftover stays alone → **odd**. "Order does not matter: odd + even is odd too."
2. "412 + 235 = 647. Even + odd = odd."
3. Cube trains (Be Curious idea, new numbers): "Start with 3 cubes and keep adding 4: 3, 7, 11, 15. Always odd! 4 is even, and odd + even = odd every time."

Your Turn: `parts`:
- `choice` "e + o will be ___" (even / odd) — e even, o odd, random 3-digit, sum ≤ 999, order of e and o random. Answer "odd".
- `num` "Start with s cubes. Add k each time: s, s+k, s+2k, ___" — s random whole number 4–21, odd or even (50/50), never 3, 7, 11 or 15 (demo numbers); k ∈ {2, 4, 6}. Answer s + 3k.

Hint: "k is even. Adding an even number does not change a leftover." Explanation computes the pattern and names the rule used (odd + even = odd, or even + even = even).

### Step 4 — The ones digits decide
Example parts:
1. "Why only the ones? 300 + 40 is groups of ten. Ten is even, so tens and hundreds never leave a leftover."
2. "627 + 154: look at 7 + 4 = 11. 11 is odd, so the sum is odd. Check: 627 + 154 = 781. Odd!"
3. "Fill the blank: odd + even = **odd**. One example: 125 + 302 = 427." (show the sentence with the blank filled, then the example equation with parity tags under each number)

Your Turn: `parts`:
- `select`-style `choice` (choices "even", "odd") for a pattern sentence randomly chosen from **seven** forms: the six book forms (`__ = even + even`, `odd + even = __`, `odd + __ = even`, `even + odd = __`, `even + __ = even`, `__ = odd + odd`) plus `even + __ = odd`. The blank may be the sum or a missing addend.
- `eqmake` (new part kind, §13): "Write one equation with 3-digit numbers that fits." Constraint per the chosen sentence (e.g. both addends odd). For a mixed sentence (one even, one odd addend) the two addends may be written in **either order**. Grading: see §13.
Hint: "Choose the word first. Then pick 3-digit numbers that end in the right digits, and add."

### Step 5 — Check a sum with patterns
Example parts:
1. "Leo writes **243 + 124 = 362**. Predict: odd + even = odd. But 362 is even. So 362 **must be wrong**. 243 + 124 = 367."
2. "Pia writes **316 + 151 = 457**. Predict: even + odd = odd. 457 is odd… but is it right? Add: 316 + 151 = **467**. A pattern can show a sum is wrong. It cannot show a sum is right. Always add to be sure."

Your Turn: `parts`, generated: a, b random 3-digit, a + b ≤ 989, true sum S ≥ 110. Claimed sum C: **25%** C = S (correct); **35%** C = S ± 1 (wrong type); **40%** C = S ± 10 or S ± 100 (right type, but wrong). C is always 3-digit (100–999).
- `choice` "Should a + b be even or odd?" (choices "even", "odd") → parity of S.
- `choice` "Is C correct?" (choices "Yes", "No") → "Yes" only when C = S, otherwise "No".
- `num` "What is a + b?" → S.
Hint: "Predict even or odd first. Then add to check."
Explanation: states predicted type, C's type, and S.

Learn reflection (`seeIt.reflection`, never graded): "How can even and odd help you check your adding?" Idea: "If the sum should be odd but you got an even number, something is wrong. If it matches, still add carefully — matching doesn't prove it."

## 10. Common mistakes and coaching

- Looking at the hundreds digit ("435 is even because 4 is even"). → Only the ones digit decides.
- Thinking odd + odd = odd. → Show two odd piles of objects; pair the leftovers.
- Believing a matching even/odd proves the sum is right (Nisha). → "The pattern can catch a mistake, but you still need to add."
- With three addends, applying a two-number rule to all three at once. → Do two numbers, then add the third.
- Writing an example equation that has the wrong types (e.g. 124 + 233 for even + even). → Check each ones digit.

## 11. Parent Guide content

- **Goal:** Tell if a sum is even or odd before adding; explain why; use it to catch adding mistakes.
- **Words:** even, odd, pattern, sum, addend, ones digit (definitions in §3).
- **Demonstrate:** (1) Use 7 coins: make pairs; one left over → odd. (2) Two piles of 5 and 3: each has a leftover; push the leftovers together → a pair → even. (3) 4 and 3 → one leftover → odd. (4) Write 627 + 154; cover everything but 7 and 4; 11 is odd, so the sum is odd; then add to check (781). (5) Write a wrong sum with the right type (316 + 151 = 457) and ask "Is it right?" — add to find 467.
- **Ask:** "What is the ones digit?" · "Will there be a leftover?" · "Does this sum look right — even or odd?" · "Does matching prove it's right?"
- **Mistakes:** see §10.
- **Checklist:** names even/odd numbers by the ones digit · knows the three patterns · explains with pairs/leftovers · writes a fitting 3-digit example · uses a pattern to reject a wrong sum and still adds to confirm · solves three-addend even/odd problems.

## 13. New interaction or model needed

1. **M-PAIRS (display only, slides):** a row of dots/cubes grouped in pairs with an optional highlighted leftover; a "join" variant shows two groups and their leftovers forming a new pair. Inputs: `pairsHTML(n)` and `pairsJoinHTML(a, b)`, n ≤ 20. Pure HTML/CSS, no interaction.
2. **M-EQMAKE — `parts` kind `eqmake` (student-made equation, shared with 2-7/2-8 ideas of "any valid answer"):** renders `[ ] + [ ] = [ ]` (three number inputs). Config: `addends: [{digits: 3, parity: 'even'|'odd'|null}, {…}]`, `anyOrder: true` (default). Correct when: each addend is a whole number with the required number of digits (100–999); the **multiset** of the two addends' parities equals the multiset required (so for a mixed sentence like "odd + even", 214 + 125 and 125 + 214 are both right; same-parity sentences are unaffected); and the typed sum equals the sum of the child's two addends. Sum may be 3 or 4 digits. `partCorrectText`: "any two [even/odd] 3-digit numbers and their correct sum, e.g. 204 + 316 = 520"; for mixed sentences "one even and one odd 3-digit number, in either order, and their correct sum". Feedback on a miss names the failed rule ("314 + 215 = 529: 215 is odd, but the sentence needs two even numbers" / "Check your adding"). **Requires cross-field grading inside one part** — the current `partOK(p, r)` already receives one part's response, so `r` should be an array of three strings for this kind.
